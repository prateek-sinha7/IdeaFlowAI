// part 01 · problem

Building a deck and delivering a talk
are two different things.

✅ A well-built deck

Consistent theme, clean layout, clear charts, restrained motion. This is the quality bar for a "static artifact."

❌ Delivering it well

Coherent logic, steady pacing, no filler words, handling questions on the fly, adjusting rhythm in the room.

💡 Where they diverge

The first is desk work; the second requires you to "glance at a slide and know what to say next."

Let me open with a claim that might be a little controversial — building a deck and delivering a talk are two completely different skills.

Look at the card on the left. What does "a well-built deck" mean? Consistent theme, clean layout, clear charts, restrained motion — these are all quality standards for a static artifact, and you can judge them offline.

But the middle card is different: "delivering it well" means coherent logic, steady pacing, no stumbling, handling questions gracefully, adjusting your rhythm based on the room — these are live-performance skills that have almost nothing to do with how good the deck looks.

And the key line is on the right — a good presenter, at their core, can "glance at a slide and know exactly what to say next." What powers that? Not memorizing a script, and not pure improvisation either — it's a well-designed teleprompter system.

// part 02 · presenter view

Presenter view needs four pieces of information

①
Current slide, large

Should take up over half the view, so a single glance tells you exactly what the audience is seeing right now.

②
Next-slide preview

Lets you prep your transition line ahead of time, instead of blanking on "wait, what's next?"

③
Speaker script area

Large type, high contrast, scrollable — this is what the presenter is actually reading.

④
Timer + slide count

Knowing how long you've talked and how many slides remain is what keeps your pacing on track.

Presenter mode should give you four pieces of information. I'll rank them by importance.

First, the current slide, large. This has to take up more than half the view, because it's your "sync anchor" with the audience — whatever they're looking at needs to be front and center in your mind too.

Second, the next-slide preview. A lot of people don't get why this matters, so let me explain: the moment a talk actually stalls isn't mid-slide, it's those two seconds right as you flip to the next one. If you can already see what's coming, the transition line writes itself.

Third, the speaker script area — this is today's main focus, and I'll go deep on it in the next section. For now, one hard requirement: the type has to be large, the contrast has to be high, and it has to scroll. Because when you're talking, you're only catching it out of the corner of your eye — if the text is small, you simply won't have time to read it.

Fourth, the timer and slide count. Knowing how long you've been talking and how many slides are left is what your pacing depends on. Keynote does this best; reveal.js's default view isn't clear enough.

All four pieces matter — none is optional. Today's deck implements all of them; press S and try it yourself.

// part 03 · script

The 3 iron rules of a speaker script

01
Not a verbatim script — a set of "prompt signals"

Bold the core points, give transition lines their own paragraph, spell out data and names clearly — so you can glance at it and pick right back up.

02
150–300 words per slide, no more, no less

Under 150 words and it won't cue you enough; over 300 and you won't have time to read it. Aim for roughly 2–3 minutes of talk per slide.

03
Write it in spoken language, not formal prose

"Therefore" → "so"; "the aforementioned approach" → "this approach." Read it out loud as you write it — it should sound like talking, not writing.

Now for the most important part — how to actually write a speaker script. I've boiled it down to 3 iron rules.

Rule one: a speaker script is not a verbatim transcript. A lot of people hear "speaker script" and assume it means writing down every word exactly as you'll say it. Wrong. If you read straight from a script, the audience notices immediately, and their trust in you evaporates.

The real job of a speaker script is to act as "prompt signals" — bold the core points, give transition lines their own paragraph, spell out data and proper nouns clearly. That way, when you're talking, one glance is enough to pick back up, but what comes out of your mouth is still your own words.

Rule two: keep each slide between 150 and 300 words. This is a number I arrived at after a dozen-plus talks. Under 150 words and you'll run out of prompts mid-sentence; over 300 and you simply won't have time to scan it all. At roughly 2 to 3 minutes per slide, this range fits perfectly.

Rule three: write it in spoken language. This is where most people trip up. If you write "therefore," what comes out of your mouth is "so"; if you write "the aforementioned approach," what comes out is "this approach." Read it out loud as you write it — if it doesn't sound natural, rewrite it.

Put these three rules together, and giving a talk suddenly becomes a lot more comfortable.

Last thing — let me walk through how to actually use this skill, so you don't have to figure it out yourself.

Step one: install the html-ppt skill, one command. Step two: copy this presenter-mode-reveal template into your own examples folder. Step three: open the HTML file and press S.

AI can help you draft it, but you should always read through it yourself out loud and make sure it sounds like something you'd actually say.
