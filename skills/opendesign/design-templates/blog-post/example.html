Why we moved our runtime to Firecracker microVMs

By Diego Restrepo · June 3, 2026 · 7 min read

For two years our container-based runtime was good enough. Then latency-sensitive teams started shipping user-facing functions, and the cold starts we'd been politely ignoring turned into support tickets we couldn't ignore.

The decision wasn't sudden. We'd been watching the cold-start distribution shift for six months before we admitted what the data was telling us. Warm invocations were great. Cold ones were a horror movie. Customers invoking rarely-hit functions on the 256MB tier were the ones writing in.

Replacing an entire function runtime sounds like the kind of project a startup is told never to do. We did it anyway. Here's how it went, what surprised us, and the parts I'd do differently.

The trigger: cold starts we couldn't fix

Container isolation is battle-tested. It is also, fundamentally, a tradeoff. Every cold invocation pulled an image, started a container, and wired up networking — and at our scale, on a spiky endpoint, that startup chain pushed the P99 cold start past 900ms.

We tried the usual fixes: holding warm capacity with min-instances, tuning the image-cache, trimming the startup path. They each helped a little. None of them got us under 300ms, and the customers we cared about needed under 50.

"We can't fix this with containers. We can fix it with something that boots in milliseconds."

Our staff engineer Priya said this in a meeting in October. She was right. The question wasn't whether to leave containers. It was what to leave them for, and how much we could keep.

What we kept; what we threw out

The CLI stayed the same. The control plane stayed the same. The bit that actually executes customer code on every invocation — that moved to Firecracker microVMs. The boundary became a single supervisor process with a small, opinionated protocol.

The numbers above are real and from production. They are also misleading without context: microVMs don't just remove the container startup chain, they also remove a layer of orchestration we'd been carrying since the first MVP.

What I'd do differently

One thing: the guest boundary. We chose jailer defaults for speed — Firecracker's tooling feels right when you want a microVM up fast. But the device setup is fiddly, and we ate two production incidents from misconfigured network namespaces before we wrote a supervisor layer that handled them once.

If I were starting today, I'd reach for firecracker-containerd or drive the VMs from a declarative spec. The lesson isn't don't use jailer; it's treat the guest boundary like a hostile edge the moment you run someone else's code.
